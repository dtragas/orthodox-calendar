Feast Days of the Orthodox Church: Pascha, the Twelve Great Feasts, and the Ranks of Saints

The Orthodox year is not a grid of empty squares — it is a procession of feasts. Every single day commemorates saints or events in the life of Christ and His Mother, and the feasts come in ranks, from the quiet memory of a local ascetic to the blazing center of the year, Pascha itself.

Pascha: the Feast of Feasts

Above every feast stands Pascha, the Resurrection of Christ — in the Church's own language not merely first among the feasts but the “Feast of Feasts and Triumph of Triumphs.” It is movable, set each year by the ancient paschal reckoning (explained in When Is Orthodox Easter?), and the entire movable cycle — Lent before, Ascension and Pentecost after — swings with it.

The Twelve Great Feasts

Below Pascha rank the Twelve Great Feasts (the Dodekaorton), celebrating the great moments in the life of Christ and of the Theotokos. Nine are fixed; three move with Pascha. Alongside the Twelve stand other great celebrations — the Circumcision of Christ and Saint Basil (January 1), the Nativity of the Baptist (June 24), Saints Peter and Paul (June 29), the Beheading of the Baptist (August 29), and the Protection of the Theotokos (October 1, especially beloved among Slavs).

Fixed and movable feasts

The calendar interlocks two cycles. The fixed cycle assigns feasts and saints to calendar dates — the same date every year. The movable cycle hangs on Pascha and shifts annually. Most name days belong to the fixed cycle, which is why a Nikos celebrates every December 6 without checking anything; a few, like Saint George's in certain years, are carried by the movable cycle instead (see Greek name days).

The ranks of feasts and saints

Not every commemoration carries the same liturgical weight, and the calendar's typography has always shown it — think of the red letters on a Greek wall calendar. Beyond the rank of the feast (great, middle, minor), the Church also distinguishes the classes of saints being honored:

  • Apostles — the Twelve, the Seventy, and Equals-to-the-Apostles like Constantine and Helen
  • Prophets — the righteous of the Old Covenant, from Moses to the Forerunner
  • Martyrs — those who witnessed with their blood; Great Martyrs like George and Demetrios among them
  • Hierarchs — sainted bishops and teachers, like Basil the Great and John Chrysostom
  • Venerable (Ὅσιοι) — monastic saints, like Anthony the Great and Mary of Egypt
  • Holy Women — the myrrhbearers, deaconesses, mothers and ascetics honored across the year
  • Righteous, Unmercenaries, Fools-for-Christ and more

Knowing the rank tells you at a glance how the day is kept: whether the Liturgy will be festal, whether the fast is relaxed, whether the village with that patron saint will hold a panegyri.
